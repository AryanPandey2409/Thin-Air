Hexacopter payload at altitude

How much payload and hover time does an 80 kg electric hexacopter lose between sea level and 6,000 m? With a fixed-voltage battery the motors can barely spin faster in thin air, so payload falls from 35 kg to about 10.5 kg at 5,000 m. Move the controls to see what sets that limit and which design changes win it back.

How it works

Method

Five pieces chain together. The atmosphere sets air density, density sets hover power and maximum thrust, and maximum thrust sets payload through the margin rule. Every input is either measured, calibrated from a public table, or a stated assumption.

1 · Atmosphere

T = 288.15 − 0.0065 h
p = 101325 (T/288.15)5.25588
ρ = p / [287.05 (T + ΔT)]

International Standard Atmosphere, troposphere. A hot or cold day shifts temperature at the same pressure altitude, so only density changes. σ = ρ / 1.225.

2 · Hover power

P = W3/2 / [FM · η · √(2ρ·N·A)]

Momentum theory with a figure of merit FM for rotor losses and η for motor and ESC losses. At fixed weight P grows as σ−1/2: +29 % at 5,000 m.

3 · Payload with margin

mpay = Tmax / (k g) − mempty − mbatt

A multirotor cannot hover at full throttle; k keeps spare thrust for climb, gusts and control. Three states: margin kept, margin short, cannot hover.

4 · Two limits

Constant power: Tmax ∝ σ1/3
Constant RPM: Tmax ∝ σ

The optimistic case lets the motor keep its sea-level shaft power. The pessimistic case pins its speed. The truth lies between.

5 · Motor and prop

T = CTρn²D⁴   Q = CQρn²D⁵
V = 2πn/Kv + I·R   I = KvQ + I0

At full throttle the pack voltage is fixed. Solving for prop speed n at each density gives Tmax. The quadratic has a closed-form root.

6 · Why it sits where it does

x = d ln T / d ln ρ = E / (E + 2 IτR)

E is back-EMF and IτR the resistive drop from torque current. Small drop: speed is pinned and x → 1. The calibrated motor gives x ≈ 0.72.

Public data

Calibration

All propulsion inputs come from T-MOTOR's published test table for its U15 II KV80 motor with a G40×13.1 carbon propeller (1.016 m) at 50 V. The test atmosphere is not stated, so ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ is assumed. Each row is reduced below; the bold row is what the model uses.

Nameplate winding resistance (17 mΩ) predicts 3,780 rpm at full throttle; the motor measured 3,193 rpm. The missing 8 V sits in the ESC, wiring and duty limit, so the model uses the measured full-throttle back-EMF fraction (0.80) instead of the datasheet resistance.

Honest scope

Limits and next steps

What the model leaves out

  • Hover only. Forward flight needs less induced power, so cruise endurance would be better.
  • Constant FM, CT and CQ. The blade Reynolds number stays above about 380,000 at 5,000 m, well clear of the range where small props degrade.
  • No rotor interference, ground effect, gusts, icing or battery voltage sag.
  • No motor thermal limit, even though cooling air thins with altitude.
  • Calibrated to one sea-level thrust-stand table. Nothing here is flight-validated.

What I would do next

  • Thrust-stand tests in a low-pressure chamber or at a high-altitude site.
  • Hover-power logging in flight to check the momentum-theory scaling.
  • Forward flight and a one-motor-out case, where equal thrust sharing no longer holds.
  • Pack discharge curves at low temperature in place of the linear capacity assumption.
About

The project

I built this to answer a question that matters for high-altitude logistics: aircraft are specified at sea level, but resupply missions fly at 4,000 m and above. A public 2025 requirement asked for about 40 kg delivered to 16,500 ft at −35 to +30 °C. This model shows why that is hard for a battery-electric multirotor and which levers help.

The aircraft is a generic heavy-lift hexacopter, not any company's product. The model is written in Python with a pytest suite of 27 checks, a notebook that regenerates every figure, and a two-page technical note. This page runs the same equations in your browser; it matches the Python model to better than 10−9.
